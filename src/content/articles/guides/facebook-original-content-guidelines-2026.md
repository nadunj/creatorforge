Meta's March 13, 2026 announcement clarified how Facebook distinguishes original content from low-value reuse. For creators building videos from existing clips, the practical issue is how much new creative value the finished piece contributes.

This article explains that dated announcement and offers an editorial workflow in response. It does not present the March update as breaking news in September.

## What Meta announced

Meta says content filmed or produced by the creator or Page owner is original. It also describes certain uses of third-party clips as original when the focus is a creator presenting substantial new information, analysis, or improvements to a storyline.

Its examples of unoriginal work include reposts and minor changes such as borders, captions, or speed adjustments. Simple facial reactions or narration that merely describes the existing footage may also be deprioritized. Meta says persistent posting of primarily unoriginal content can affect recommendation eligibility and monetization, and creators can appeal originality decisions. [Source: Meta's March 2026 announcement](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/03/rewarding-original-creators-on-facebook/).

The company also published separate information about its Creator Fast Track program on March 18, 2026, aimed at creators bringing an established audience to Facebook. That program is distinct from the originality guidance; a news story about one should not imply everyone automatically qualifies for the other. [Source: Meta Creator Fast Track announcement](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/03/creator-fast-track-grow-your-audience-earn-money-on-facebook/).

## Start with a question the video will answer

An effective editorial approach is to write the video's main question before gathering clips. For example, a small-business page could ask why two product photographs produce different results. The creator can then demonstrate lighting choices, show their own comparison, and explain the tradeoffs.

That planning process gives the video a reason to exist beyond the availability of an attention-grabbing clip. It also helps the editor identify footage that does not contribute to the explanation.

## Review the script without the borrowed footage

Read the narration as a standalone document. Highlight the observations, demonstrations, and conclusions that came from your own work. If very little remains, develop the explanation before spending more time on transitions or captions.

This is an editorial exercise, not a formula for passing a platform review. There is no promised ratio of original words to borrowed footage in this article.

For a commentary video, outline the sequence as question, evidence, interpretation, and conclusion. Keep a source note beside each factual claim. For a tutorial, save the demonstration files so you can reproduce the result if a viewer asks how it was made.

## Organize the production evidence

Keep raw recordings, project files, script revisions, and permissions together. Use filenames that connect the final upload to its source material. This makes future corrections, client handoffs, and your own editorial review easier.

When reviewing an existing content calendar, prioritize ideas that can be developed through your own demonstrations or analysis. Avoid treating a cosmetic edit as the main creative contribution.

The workable response is to make your contribution clear during planning, then carry it through the script and edit. That is a production recommendation based on the announcement, not a guarantee of reach, income, or account eligibility.
