Starlink's next-generation satellite plans focus on adding network capacity. For YouTubers uploading large files, designers transferring client work, and businesses operating away from dependable fixed broadband, the useful question is what those plans mean for a real connection.

Here are the published developments to understand, followed by the checks to make before ordering. This article was researched on September 5, 2026. Announced capacity and regulatory permission should not be read as a promise that a specific upgrade is already active at your address.

## V3 is designed to carry more traffic

Starlink's published V3 overview describes a satellite design supporting 1 terabit per second of downlink capacity and 160 gigabits per second of uplink capacity. The company compares these with roughly ten times the downlink and twenty-two times the uplink capacity of its V2 satellite. It also describes upgraded antennas and additional communication beams. [Source: Starlink V3 overview](https://starlink.com/updates/starlink-version-3-satellites).

Those are satellite capacity figures. They are not the download or upload speed promised to one household. A creator should therefore avoid treating a "1 Tbps" headline as a residential plan specification.

Additional shared capacity could matter to creators if it translates into better service where they work. That outcome still needs to be assessed through the local offer and actual performance, rather than inferred from the satellite specification alone.

## The FCC approved another 7,500 Gen2 satellites

On January 9, 2026, the US Federal Communications Commission authorized an additional 7,500 second-generation Starlink satellites, bringing the authorized Gen2 total covered by those grants to 15,000. Its announcement also described additional orbital shells and frequency permissions. [Source: FCC authorization announcement](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-417881A1.pdf).

This is a regulatory milestone, not a count of satellites already operating. It also does not mean every country automatically has retail service available. The V3 design name and the FCC's Gen2 regulatory terminology describe different aspects of the system and should not be substituted for one another.

## What global creators should check before ordering

Begin with your exact service address in Starlink's availability and ordering pages. Review the plan offered there, its hardware requirements, and the full cost shown for your location. Do not apply a price quoted in another country to your own budget. [Source: Starlink availability map](https://starlink.com/map).

Then list the work your connection must support. Include a typical video upload, a client video call, cloud backups, and any live broadcasts. Record your current connection's behavior during the hours you normally work so you have a useful comparison.

For a simple planning example, a 5 GB file contains approximately 40 gigabits of data. At a sustained 20 Mbps upload rate, the theoretical transfer time is about 33 minutes before overhead and interruptions. This is arithmetic using an assumed rate, not a Starlink speed test or performance claim.

Check the installation location as part of the decision. Starlink's guidance explains that trees, buildings, and other obstructions can interrupt the path to a satellite. Use its setup guidance to assess the proposed position before choosing mounting equipment. [Source: Starlink obstruction guidance](https://starlink.com/ck/support/article/bcbf0078-be81-d345-4bce-ebbcfa196f56).
